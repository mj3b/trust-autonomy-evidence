## The problem this crosswalk addresses

Governance instruments can assign oversight duties without producing evidence that a person could influence a particular decision. A policy may require a reviewer, a notice, an audit, or a stop control. Each requirement addresses part of governance. The institutional claim becomes stronger only when records show that the person received usable information, understood the system and situation, had authority and time to act, exercised judgment, and changed execution.

The practical-human-control method tests that connection. This crosswalk identifies where selected instruments touch the six-stage chain and states what the present research still cannot conclude.

### Manuscript-ready central point

The EU AI Act and major assurance frameworks describe capabilities and processes that institutions should establish. They do not, by their existence alone, show that those arrangements had practical force in one decision. The proposed method supplies a separate evidentiary question: what records show that assigned oversight became timely, informed, exercised, and propagated control?

This relationship matters because a documented role can satisfy an administrative checkpoint while leaving the intervention path untested. The six-stage chain turns the general oversight claim into linked propositions. A failure records evidence that at least one required condition was absent. An unresolved result records a surviving evidence gap. A pass requires support for every required stage and remains bounded to the defined event.

## Regulatory sandbox research use

An EU AI Act sandbox can test the method prospectively because the study can define the decision boundary before the event and capture the records that historical reconstruction often lacks. The study should preregister the six required stages, the timing-margin inputs, the intervention interface, the classification rules, and the conclusion boundary. It should then preserve:

1. the exact output and supporting information shown to the overseer;
2. system version, operating state, uncertainty, and known limitations;
3. operator role, training basis, and tested intervention rights;
4. timestamps for access, interpretation, decision, transmission, propagation, and irreversible commitment;
5. the contemporaneous decision, challenge, override, stop, or escalation record;
6. evidence that the intervention reached downstream execution;
7. missing records and the retention rule that governed them;
8. correction, repair, and reform records when claims extend beyond the event.

The first prospective study should be described as a method test. A solo run can test whether the protocol and artifact pipeline function. A blinded second assessor is required for a reliability claim. Field validity also requires sampling across the intended systems and tasks and comparison with an external criterion that is independent of the six-stage labels.
